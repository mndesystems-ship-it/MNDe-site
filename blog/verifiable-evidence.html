Evidence you can verify without trusting us

Two linked receipts — what was authorized, and what was executed — checkable offline against published keys. The design goal is to make our word unnecessary.

Most audit trails ask you to trust the system that produced them. The log says the right thing happened; believing it means believing the logger was honest and uncompromised at the moment it wrote. For low-stakes bookkeeping that is fine. For authority over consequential actions, “trust our log” is exactly the assumption you were trying to avoid. Better evidence is evidence you can check yourself, offline, without the issuer in the loop.

Two receipts, cryptographically linked

The model is deliberately simple. An authorization receipt records the decision: MNDe authorized execution X under policy P using grant G. An execution receipt records the act: executor Y used grant G for execution X and observed result Z. Each is independently signed. They are bound together by execution id, grant hash, and result hash, so you cannot hold up one without the other, and you cannot alter either without breaking the link.

Given the two receipts and the published trust roots, a verifier can confirm — with no live connection to us — that a specific action was authorized under a specific policy, that it was the exact action executed, and that the observed result is the one bound into the record. A broken link or an invalid signature fails verification. There is no “trust me” step left to lean on.

Design goal

Evidence should be verifiable against published keys, offline, by someone who does not trust the party that produced it. If checking the record requires trusting the recorder, it is not yet evidence.

Where we draw the honest line

Signed, linked receipts get you tamper-evidence: you can tell if the record was altered. They do not, by themselves, get you tamper-resistance against a party that simply withholds or presents a split view of history. Closing that requires independent witnesses and a transparency log with inclusion and consistency proofs — turning “trust the log” into “verify the log against observers who don't collude.” We treat that witnessing layer as target architecture, and we say so rather than imply it already ships.

Why verifiable evidence is becoming the ask

As more consequential actions are taken by software, “show me it was authorized” stops being an internal nicety and starts being something counterparties, auditors, and regulators will reasonably expect. Systems that can hand over independently checkable evidence — rather than a log you have to take on faith — will have a much easier decade. Building for that expectation now is cheaper than retrofitting it under pressure later.
