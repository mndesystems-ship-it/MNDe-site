Single-use by default: authority that cannot be replayed

A grant you can present twice is a grant you have already lost control of. If the same token authorizes the same action every time it is shown, then a copy of it — leaked, logged, or intercepted — is as good as the original. Single-use is not a hardening option to add later; it is the sane default for authority over consequential acts.

Reusable means replayable

The word "replay" makes it sound exotic. It is not. Reusable authority is replayable authority, by definition: the value works more than once, so anyone who obtains the value can make it work again. A bearer token that grants an action on every presentation is a small, portable instruction that says "do this," and it will say it to whoever repeats it. The convenience of reuse and the risk of replay are the same property viewed from two sides.

One nonce, one action, consumed once

Single-use authority carries a nonce — a value that is valid exactly once. The grant is bound to that nonce and to one specific action. Before the protected executor performs the action, it reserves and consumes the nonce; after that, the nonce is spent. A second presentation of the same grant finds a consumed nonce and is refused. The authority did its one job and then ceased to be authority.

Pair single-use with a short expiration and the exposure window collapses further. A grant that is valid once, for a specific act, for a brief moment, is a poor thing to steal. There is little to replay and almost no time in which to try.

A grant is consumed exactly once; a second presentation is refused as replay.

The hard part is "exactly once"

Saying a grant is single-use is easy. Guaranteeing it under concurrency is the real engineering. If two executors present the same grant at the same instant, exactly one must win the reservation and the other must lose — cleanly, with no window in which both believe they succeeded. That is a linearizability property, and it belongs to the consumption ledger, not to hopeful application code.

We are deliberate about how strong a claim we make here. A single-process reference can demonstrate one-winner selection, and ours does. Demonstrating it as a distributed, linearizable guarantee — the version that survives real infrastructure, partitions, and retries — is target architecture, and proving it requires a deployed ledger under an adversarial test, not a diagram. We would rather name that gap than paper over it.

Why this is the default, not a mode

If authority is reusable by default and single-use is an opt-in, the safe path is the one people forget to take. Making single-use the default inverts that: reuse becomes the thing you must consciously and narrowly justify, and the ordinary grant is one that cannot be replayed because it no longer exists after it is used. That default is what lets "present valid, specific, unconsumed authority" mean something at the moment of execution.
