Thesis · 18 Aug 2026

Capability, not identity, is the unit of machine trust

Identity answers who is asking. It never answers whether this exact action, on this exact resource, right now, was authorized. As the principals taking actions stop being people, that gap stops being academic.

Most access control still answers a question from a human-shaped world: who are you, and what role do you hold? Prove the identity, look up the role, and a broad bundle of standing permissions follows. That model has carried us a long way. It was designed for a small number of durable principals, acting slowly, who could be held accountable after the fact.

Autonomous software breaks all three assumptions at once. The principals are numerous and short-lived. They act in milliseconds, in bursts, across systems no single reviewer watches. And “accountable after the fact” is cold comfort when the fact is an irreversible merge, transfer, or deployment. Identity tells you the request came from a credential you once trusted. It does not tell you the action in front of you is the action you meant to allow.

The question identity cannot answer

Consider a credential scoped to “can merge pull requests.” It authenticates. It even authorizes, in the coarse sense. But it says nothing about which pull request, which head commit, which base branch, or whether this is the first time the action has been attempted. A leaked or confused agent holding that credential is, permission-wise, indistinguishable from a legitimate one. The system has no place to stand.

Capability inverts the model. Instead of asking who you are and inferring what you may do, it requires you to present authority for the specific act: a signed exact-action grant that names one action, one resource, a canonical digest of its parameters, a short expiration, and a single-use nonce. The unit of trust is not the actor. It is the act.

Invariant

No consequential machine action executes unless it presents valid, specific, unconsumed authority for that exact action. Identity is an input to issuing that authority — never a substitute for it.

Why the shift is not optional for long

It is tempting to treat exact-action authority as belt-and-suspenders — nice for the highest risk paths, overkill everywhere else. We think that framing has a shelf life. Every trend in how software acts points the same way: more autonomous principals, more delegation between them, more actions taken without a human in the loop to sanity-check the last mile. Each of those makes ambient, identity-shaped permission a little more dangerous and a little harder to reason about.

We are not predicting a dramatic break. We are describing a slope. The organizations that will be comfortable operating fleets of agents are the ones that, quietly and early, moved the unit of trust from “who” to “which exact action” — and built the evidence to prove it.

What this looks like in practice

  • Authority is minted per action, not held as a standing role.
  • The parameters that were authorized are bound cryptographically, so they cannot drift.
  • A grant is consumed exactly once; a second presentation is refused, not honored.
  • Every decision leaves a receipt that can be verified without trusting the issuer.

None of this removes identity — you still need to know who may request authority. It relocates the real decision to the moment of action, where the stakes actually are.
