Capability, not identity, is the unit of machine trust

Identity answers a question — who is asking — that was never the question a consequential action needed answered. As the principals in our systems stop being people and start being programs, the gap between "who" and "what, exactly, is allowed" becomes the whole problem.

Identity and authority were quietly conflated

For most of the history of access control, authenticating a caller and authorizing an action were close enough to treat as one step. A person logged in, and the system trusted that the human on the other end would exercise judgment about what to do next. Roles and scopes stood in for that judgment: give the operator the "deploy" role and trust them to deploy the right thing, at the right time, for the right reason.

That worked because a person was in the loop, absorbing the ambiguity. Identity was a proxy for a whole context of intent that lived in someone's head. Remove the person and the proxy stops standing for anything. A program holding the "deploy" role has no judgment to fall back on; it has exactly the authority the token encodes, applied at machine speed, across every path that token can reach.

A key is not a decision

Authentication proves possession of a credential. It says the caller is who the credential belongs to. It says nothing about whether this request — this repository, this commit, this amount, this resource, right now — was ever intended. A valid token on an unintended action is still a valid token. The system sees a known principal and waves it through, because "known principal" is all it was ever built to check.

The failure mode this produces is familiar: an agent is provisioned with broad, standing credentials so it can handle whatever comes up, and then it does something no one decided to allow — not because the credential was stolen, but because the credential was always broader than any single action required.

Capabilities name the act, not the actor

A capability inverts the default. Instead of "this actor may do things in this category," it says "this exact action, against this exact resource, with these exact parameters, is authorized — once, briefly." The unit of trust is the act, bound to the authority that permits it, rather than a durable badge the actor carries around.

Identity does not disappear; it becomes an input to issuing a capability rather than the thing checked at execution. The question shifts from "do we recognize you?" to "do you hold valid, specific, unconsumed authority for the thing you are about to do?"

No consequential machine action executes unless it presents valid, specific, unconsumed authority for that exact action.

What this changes in practice

Concretely, it means a compromised or confused agent cannot convert broad standing access into an unintended act, because it never holds broad standing access to convert. It holds — at most — a grant for the specific thing it was authorized to do, and that grant does not generalize. This is the boundary MNDe is built around. Today it is demonstrated on a single reference integration; treating it as a universal control across every provider an organization uses is target architecture, and we try to say so plainly wherever the claim appears.

Capability over identity is not a rejection of identity. It is a recognition that, for non-human principals, identity was answering the easy question and leaving the hard one to a human who is no longer there.
