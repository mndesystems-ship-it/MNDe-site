Preparing for a world of many agents

The count of non-human principals taking consequential actions only goes one direction. Controls that feel optional at one agent become load-bearing at ten thousand.

It is worth stating plainly what the rest of these notes assume: the number of autonomous software principals taking real actions — merging code, moving money, provisioning infrastructure, calling other systems — is growing, and we don't see the trend reversing. You can disagree about the pace. The direction is hard to argue with. And a change in the number of actors is not a quantitative detail; past a certain point it changes which controls are viable.

Small numbers hide bad assumptions

When one carefully supervised agent holds a credential, a lot of shortcuts look fine. Broad scopes are convenient. Reusable tokens are simpler. “We'll review the logs if something looks off” is a plausible plan, because a human can actually watch one thing. None of those assumptions survive contact with a fleet. Broad scopes multiply blast radius. Reusable tokens multiply replay surface. And no one reviews the logs of ten thousand principals acting every second — there is no one positioned in that last mile at all.

This is the pattern behind everything else we've written: capability over identity, authority at the moment of execution, single-use by default, evidence you can verify without trusting the issuer. Each is a modest, almost fussy discipline at the scale of one agent. Each becomes structurally necessary at the scale we expect. The controls don't change. Their optionality does.

The bet

Authorization for machine actions will follow the path other security primitives took: from optional, to expected, to assumed. The comfortable operators of large agent fleets will be the ones who moved early — quietly making exact-action authority the default.

Infrastructure, not a feature

We think machine authorization ends up looking less like a product feature and more like plumbing — the way a certificate authority, an HSM, or an identity provider is plumbing. Not something every team reinvents per application, but a shared authority plane that issues exact-action grants, a protected execution path that consumes them, and a verifiable evidence trail underneath. That is the shape we are building toward, and we are deliberate about marking what is implemented today, what is limited, and what is still target architecture.

We are not claiming to know exactly how this settles. What we are confident about is the pressure: more autonomous actions, taken faster, with fewer humans in the loop, against systems where the wrong action is expensive to undo. In that world, “the agent had permission” will not be an acceptable answer. “This exact action presented valid, specific, unconsumed authority, and here is the evidence” will be.

What to do before you need it

  • Find the handful of actions that are genuinely irreversible, and require exact-action authority there first.
  • Treat reusable, broadly-scoped credentials as debt to pay down, not a permanent baseline.
  • Make sure “prove it was authorized” has an answer that doesn't depend on trusting your own log.

None of that requires betting on a specific future. It just requires taking the direction seriously a little earlier than you strictly have to.
